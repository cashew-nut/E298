One problem, one approach. CRAs spend a lot of their time piecing together the picture of each site from different systems, documents and conversations. I’d fix that without building another application. Four parts: the opportunity, my recommendation, making it real, and how I used AI.

CRAs spend hours piecing the site picture together

$33–46M of CRA time wasted each year on monitoring inefficiencies at the largest pharmaceutical organisations Scaled from Veeva’s benchmark of 30+ biopharma companies over 10 years (vendor research)
How this number is built
  1. Veeva’s benchmark: 1,286 working days wasted per 1,000 monitoring visit reports. Veeva, Clinical Data Industry Research, September 2025
  2. A CRA earns about $75k a year, blended globally ($80k to $100k in the US): about $40 an hour over 1,875 working hours. So each report wastes about $410 of CRA time. Assumption, salary only
  3. The largest sponsors run about 400 ongoing studies a year. ClinicalTrials.gov counts, 2024 to 2026
  4. Assume about 40 active sites per study on average, across all phases: about 16,000 sites. Assumption
  5. Sites are visited about every 7 weeks, fewer under risk-based monitoring: 5 to 7 visits a site a year, so 80,000 to 112,000 visit reports. Tufts CSDD CRA survey, DIA Global Forum, January 2026
  6. 80,000 to 112,000 reports at about $410 each is $33M to $46M a year.
  7. Check: that many visits keeps about 1,000 CRAs busy at 7.9 visits a month. Tufts CSDD, 2013

Not another app

Everything a CRA juggles, pulled into the workspace they already use.

Press the right arrow to open it
Not another app. Everything a CRA juggles today, from email, SharePoint and Teams to the study systems, comes into the workspace the company already runs, such as Copilot, Cowork, Google Workspace or Codex. Press right to open it. So what if the workspace a CRA already uses did that piecing together for them? Here’s what that looks like before a visit. It’s a mock-up, and everything in it is illustrative: the study, the site, the subjects and the documents. Press the right arrow to step through it. The workspace they already use opens on a notice: it has seen the upcoming visit in CTMS and offers to prepare it. The CRA says yes. The agent reads the source systems, matches the subject across them, applies shared definitions, and runs deterministic signal checks. The next slide shows what makes that possible. What comes back is a short brief, riskiest first. The top item is a possible protocol deviation: IRT has subject 007 in the 65 and over stratum, but EDC gives an age of 63 at consent. The agent checks the protocol, shows the page it relied on and links to the source document. Then it pulls the deviation plan section: important if confirmed, five working days to record it, don’t re-randomise. Draft deviation record opens the CTMS form, already filled in from the signal, the protocol and the plan, for the CRA to review and create. Rules raise the signal, AI finds the evidence, and the CRA confirms at the visit and signs anything that’s filed.

None of that is a new app. It works because of what sits underneath, and that’s the key choice I’d make. Start with the foundation, from the bottom up. Source systems stay as they are. The data layer conforms them to standards, with master and reference data. I’m assuming that exists; if it doesn’t, we build it first. The semantic layer gives the data business meaning. The tools layer holds the CRA skills and tools, such as deterministic signal checks on a CRA’s assigned sites, served as MCP tools on a managed gateway to one agent rather than a set of agents. And it surfaces in the enterprise workspaces CRAs already use, Copilot or Cowork. The middle layers we build or buy. Access by role and policy wraps every layer. Click a layer, or the access label, for an example. Then click Another app for the alternative: one app per problem, a login and training for each, and in the end everyone still runs the site from a spreadsheet. Then the trade: we give up a quick start and complete control of the interface. We get context, simplicity, and scale both ways.

What comes out of it

    No funding ask on day one. First, executive champions across clinical operations leadership, including heads of both site and centralised monitoring. Through them, find CRA champions, interview them, and put a two-week prototype on synthetic data in front of them. If it holds up, ask for sponsorship and funding, with a decision checkpoint at six months. If we go ahead, pilot on a few early-adopter studies across therapeutic areas, with an adoption plan. Quality and AI governance comes in during the two-week sprint, not while I’m finding champions. I’d talk IT and business quality managers through the design and work with them so the approach is proportionate to the risk of this context of use. And it’s complementary: it works with the monitoring tools sponsors already run and retires the side trackers around them. The pilot starts read-only, with no change to the monitoring plan, so it’s an easy yes. Through the build, the evidence scales with that risk, so we’re GxP qualified by the six-month decision and the pilot starts on a qualified system.

    How we’d know it’s valuable and safe

    Next reveals the next set

    Three sets of measures, read at different times. Leading indicators, reviewed weekly, tell us early whether CRAs trust it: are they prepared, do they act on what it shows, do they keep using it. Lagging indicators, reviewed monthly, justify scaling: issues resolved faster, fewer asks of sites, fewer unplanned visits, lower cost. Safety is a gate, not a trend, reviewed prior to each release: evals pass, no more missed signals than today, every claim cites a source, and nothing leaves the workspace without a person signing it. These are suggestions to agree with the team, not targets I’m claiming.

    Appendix, if asked about the business case. It’s a simple model, and every number is an assumption. Costs: build, run, validation, and change and training, about $19.5 million over three years.
